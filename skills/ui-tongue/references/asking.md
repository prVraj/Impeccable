# How to ask

Read this at step 3, with `interview.md`. That file says what to ask; this one says how to ask so the answer is real.

## Why people answer badly, and what each rule here fixes

| What happens | Why it happens | What you do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| They can't say what they like | People recognise far better than they recall. Asked cold, they reach for mood words ("clean", "vibe") | Show, then ask: named sites, candidate lists, two options side by side |
| They say yes to whatever you recommend | Agreeing is the easiest answer when a question feels like a quiz (survey researchers call it acquiescence) | Every inferred item comes with its closest alternative, so "yes" is a choice between two things |
| Later answers get shorter and lazier | Each question costs effort; people start doing the least that counts as an answer (satisficing) | Fewer, sharper questions; skip what the references already answer; one round per message |
| They tell you the surface, not the reason | The first answer is usually an attribute ("the big type"); the reason sits one level down | One "why" follow-up when the reason decides the rule |
| A follow-up chain feels like an interrogation | Repeated whys feel like being tested | At most one follow-up per question; stop as soon as the answer settles what the question's why needs |

## The three moves

**Show, then ask.** Put the options in front of them as named, visible things: "Linear (tight, quiet sans), Stripe (big and confident), Apple (huge and centred), or an editorial serif?" Never "what font weight do you like?".

**One rung down.** When an answer names a thing without its reason, ask once: "What does that do for you?" or "What would feel wrong if it were different?". The reason becomes the rule's why and tells you how far to stretch it. One rung only.
Example: "I like Stripe's gradients." "What do they do for you?" "They make it feel premium without shouting." The rule becomes "shade-to-shade gradients in one hue, never rainbow; premium, not loud", not "use gradients".

**Two alike, one different.** When they can't put a feeling into words, pick three of their references and ask: "Which two of these feel most alike, and what makes the third different?" The difference they name is a real axis of their taste (dense vs. airy, warm vs. cold, quiet vs. loud), in their own words.
Use it at most once per interview, in Round 1 or 2, when Q3 answers stay vague after the candidate list.

## Offering choices

- Two to four options, each a thing they can picture. Always a last option: "something else: tell me".
- Your recommendation goes after the options, with its reference ("Linear and Vercel, the two sites you picked for colour") or "my default".
- In the round checklist, the alternative is the nearest real choice, not a straw man: "8px corners or sharp 2px", not "8px corners or no corners at all".

## Words to listen for

| They say | It usually means | You do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Clean" | Few elements, generous space, low contrast lines | Show candidates: air, hairlines, muted colour, one accent. Let them pick |
| "Modern" | Whatever is current in their references | Ask which reference feels most modern, then drill that one |
| "Premium" | Restraint plus one rich detail (depth, type, motion) | Ask which detail carries it |
| "Fun" | Colour, bounce, or playful copy | Two alike, one different, with a playful and a calm reference |
| "Like Apple" | Usually big type and calm motion, rarely the layout | Bucket list for Apple, as for any site |
| "Everything" | They haven't looked closely | Show what you found on that site; pick from it |

Done when every answer settles what its question's why needs, without a second follow-up.
